NTUTEC
Founder Pipeline.

I single-handedly built the web app NTUTEC’s founder programs run on. It follows founders from their first open meetup into the courses and consultations of NTU Garage, our incubator, and our accelerator. Instead of a separate snapshot of each founder at every event, the center now sees their whole path and where they get stuck.

Designing the programs.

I designed the 2026 edition of NTU Garage for its 20 early-stage teams: goal-setting with OKRs, four validation workshops and specialist group consultations, delivered with mentors and colleagues.

Innovation Support opens an earlier door: no company, pitch deck or university affiliation is needed. I launched it as monthly meetups that opened the center’s support to anyone for the first time in its 13 years.

Running the programs.

For the center’s staff, each team’s live progress, consultation records and next steps sit in one place. A rules engine manages every course and consultation across the incubator and accelerator, closes session to-dos automatically and flags teams that miss three sessions in a row.

Innovation Support.

Free monthly meetups, open to anyone. I designed two ways to participate: a public board for finding partners and product testers, and a separate question box for asking for help.

A public board and an anonymous question box.

Founders often worry that asking for help looks like weakness. Networking posts go on the public board; requests for help go to a separate question box. The big-screen response omits the author, while the host can see who asked and follow up.

One record that grows over time.

With consent, each attendee’s background, needs, attendance and feedback build into a single record that follows them from the meetups into our programs, including whether they later register interest in applying.

Travel Fox.
Three places, one itinerary.

On a trip abroad, the plan, the spending in another currency and the tips friends later ask for usually sit in three places. Travel Fox keeps them in one itinerary: planned costs become expenses automatically, and the whole trip becomes a single link instead of Google Maps links pasted one by one. I built it with Claude Code after my own trip to Japan and opened it to anyone.

A private trip should not mean a broken link.

Each trip can be private, link-only or public. Users wanted to change a trip’s visibility without losing its sharing link, so I made the link stay with the trip: while the trip is private, visitors see a private-trip message; when sharing is reopened, the same link works again.

Two Second Brain concept pages, on the jagged technological frontier and the ironies of automation, reshaped my first 1→0 article draft for engineers. They shifted its focus from faster output to the judgement still needed to assess it.

The draft connects an AI study with consultants to earlier work on automation, while making the limits of applying those findings to engineers explicit.

A lesson from BeSci Wiki.

When a key-term tagging score jumped from 0 to 0.94, I checked the answer key before trusting it: it covered only 12 of the 79 terms. I stopped treating that score as evidence of overall quality and halted the full re-extraction that depended on it. That earlier experiment is why I examine what an evaluation actually covers.

Forget About Age

As CEO, I led a 6-person core team building an intergenerational camp service that pairs college students with older adults. We sold a two-day camp to NSYSU’s senior learning program and designed it with participants through a World Café co-design workshop.

In the post-camp survey I ran, 95% of respondents would return (19 of 20), and the overall rating was 4.55/5.

What the feedback changed

Student hosts received the highest rating, 4.95/5, above the therapeutic activities we had built the camp around. We proposed a shorter format centred on student hosts, simpler activities and host training for the next camp.
